What to do when the power goes out

A step-by-step power outage checklist: the first 15 minutes, keeping food safe, running a generator without risking carbon monoxide, and what to do when the power comes back.

By the BugOut team. Updated November 3, 2026. Checked against Ready.gov, USDA, CDC, OSHA and U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission guidance.

The short answer
  1. Check whether it's just your home, then report the outage to your utility.
  2. Keep the fridge and freezer closed: food stays safe about 4 hours in the fridge, 48 hours in a full freezer and 24 hours in a half-full one.
  3. Use flashlights, not candles, and unplug electronics so a power surge can't damage them.
  4. Run generators, grills and camp stoves outdoors only, at least 20 feet from windows, doors and vents, with the exhaust pointed away from the house. Never heat your home with a gas oven or stove.
  5. If it gets dangerously hot or cold, go somewhere with power, and check on older neighbors.

The first 15 minutes

  1. Check if it's only you. Look at your breaker panel and whether the neighbors' lights are on. A tripped breaker is a much easier fix.
  2. Report it. Use your utility's app, website or outage line, and note the estimated restoration time. It tells you whether to plan for hours or days.
  3. Switch to flashlights. Candles are a fire risk, especially with kids or pets moving around in the dark.
  4. Unplug appliances and electronics. Power can return with surges that damage them. Leave one lamp switched on so you know when it's back.
  5. Close the fridge and freezer. Every opening lets cold out. Decide what you need before you open the door.
  6. Save your phone battery. Turn on Low Power Mode, dim the screen, and text rather than call; texts get through more easily when networks are busy.
  7. Start the backup plan for medical equipment. If anyone relies on oxygen, a CPAP or refrigerated medicine, act now rather than waiting to see how long the outage lasts.

If you have to drive, treat dark traffic signals as four-way stops.

Keeping food safe

The USDA's numbers assume the doors stay closed:

WhereFood stays safe for
RefrigeratorUp to 4 hours
Full freezerUp to 48 hours
Half-full freezerUp to 24 hours
  • After 4 hours without power, throw out refrigerated meat, poultry, fish, eggs, milk, soft cheese and leftovers. Ready.gov's rule: discard any perishable food that has been at 40°F or warmer for two hours or more.
  • Keep an appliance thermometer in the fridge and freezer so you know the real temperature when the power comes back.
  • Frozen food that still has ice crystals or is at 40°F or colder can be refrozen or cooked.
  • For a long outage, move food to coolers with ice. The USDA says 50 pounds of dry ice should hold an 18-cubic-foot freezer for about 2 days. Don't touch dry ice with bare hands.
  • Never taste food to check whether it's safe. When in doubt, throw it out.

Generators and carbon monoxide

Carbon monoxide from portable generators alone kills more than 80 people a year in the U.S., according to the Consumer Product Safety Commission. It's invisible, has no smell, and builds up from anything that burns fuel. If the carbon monoxide alarm sounds, or anyone has a headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea or confusion, get everyone outside into fresh air right away, then call 911. Several people or pets feeling sick at once is a strong sign.

  • Run a generator outdoors only, at least 20 feet from doors, windows and vents, the distance the CDC gives, with the exhaust pointed away from the house. Never in a house, garage, basement, crawl space or shed, or on a porch, even with the doors open.
  • Never use a grill, camp stove, charcoal or an outdoor propane or kerosene heater indoors, and never heat your home with a gas oven or stovetop.
  • Never run a car in a garage, even with the garage door open.
  • Put battery-powered or battery-backup carbon monoxide alarms on every level of your home, near bedrooms.
  • Never plug a generator into a wall outlet or connect it to your home's wiring without a transfer switch installed by an electrician. OSHA warns this can energize power lines and electrocute utility workers. Otherwise, plug appliances into the generator with heavy-duty outdoor extension cords.

Signs of carbon monoxide poisoning include headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea or vomiting, chest pain and confusion. People who are asleep or have been drinking can die before they notice anything.

Staying warm in a winter outage

  • Pick one small room, ideally with few windows, and keep everyone in it. Close the doors to other rooms.
  • Block drafts under doors with towels, hang blankets over windows at night, and open the curtains on sunny windows during the day.
  • Wear loose layers, a hat and dry socks, and share blankets and body heat.
  • Eat regular meals and drink warm, non-alcoholic drinks.
  • Let faucets drip so pipes don't freeze.
  • If you can't keep one room warm, go to a warming center or a home with heat. Babies and older adults lose heat fastest.

Staying cool in a summer outage

  • Spend the hottest hours somewhere with air conditioning: a cooling center, library or store.
  • Drink water before you're thirsty, close the blinds on sunny windows, and take cool showers if you still have water.
  • Never leave anyone, or a pet, in a parked car.
  • Heat stroke is an emergency: a body temperature of 103°F or higher, hot red skin, confusion or passing out. Call 911, move the person somewhere cooler and cool them with cool water or wet cloths while you wait. Don't give them anything to drink.

Medicines and medical devices

  • Talk to your doctor now about a plan for powered equipment and refrigerated medicines.
  • Insulin and some other medicines must stay cool but not frozen. The package insert says how long each keeps at room temperature.
  • Keep charged backup batteries for oxygen concentrators, CPAPs and power wheelchairs, and know where you'll go if the outage runs long.
  • Ask your utility whether it has a medical priority or medical certificate program for customers who depend on electricity.

When the power comes back

  • Wait a few minutes, then plug appliances back in one at a time.
  • Check the fridge and freezer thermometers and go through the food rules above before eating anything.
  • Reset clocks and alarms, and recharge every battery and power bank.
  • Restock whatever you used, so you're ready for the next one.

Power outage kit checklist

Keep these together where you can find them in the dark.

  • A flashlight or headlamp for each person, plus extra batteries
  • A battery-powered or hand-crank radio
  • Charged power banks and a car phone charger
  • Battery-powered carbon monoxide alarms
  • Appliance thermometers for the fridge and freezer
  • A cooler and frozen ice packs or jugs of frozen water
  • Water: 1 gallon per person per day for at least 3 days, 2 weeks if you canThe CDC's recommendation. Store more for hot weather, pregnancy, illness and pets.
  • At least 3 days of food that needs no cooking, and a manual can opener
  • A first-aid kit and a supply of everyone's medicines
  • Warm blankets or sleeping bags
  • Cash in small bills
  • A paper list of phone numbers, including your utility's outage line
  • A generator only if you can run it outdoors, at least 20 feet from the house, with the right cords

Quick answers

How long does food last in the fridge when the power goes out?

About 4 hours if you keep the door closed. A full freezer keeps food safe for about 48 hours and a half-full one for about 24 hours, according to the USDA. Throw out perishable food that has been at 40°F or warmer for two hours or more.

How far from the house should a generator be?

Outdoors and at least 20 feet from doors, windows and vents, the distance the CDC gives, with the exhaust pointed away from the house. Never run one in a house, garage, basement, shed or on a porch, even with the doors open.

What should you not do during a power outage?

Don't run a generator, grill or camp stove indoors or in a garage, don't heat your home with a gas oven or stove, don't connect a generator to your home's wiring without a transfer switch, don't open the fridge more than you need to, and use flashlights rather than candles.

What should be in a power outage kit?

Flashlights and extra batteries, a battery or hand-crank radio, charged power banks, battery-powered carbon monoxide alarms, appliance thermometers, a cooler, water (1 gallon per person per day for at least 3 days), no-cook food, a first-aid kit and medicines, warm blankets and some cash.
