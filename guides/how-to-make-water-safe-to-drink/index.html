How to make water safe to drink

Boiling, bleach, tablets and filters, with the exact times and doses from the EPA and CDC, plus where to find water in the wild and at home, and how much to store.

By the BugOut team. Updated November 3, 2026. Checked against EPA, CDC and Minnesota Department of Health guidance.

The short answer

Boil it. Bring clear water to a rolling boil for 1 minute, or 3 minutes at high elevation, then let it cool covered. If you can't boil it, use plain unscented bleach: 8 drops per gallon of 6% bleach or 6 drops of 8.25% bleach, doubled if the water is cloudy. Stir and wait 30 minutes. If the water is cloudy to begin with, let it settle and pour it through a cloth or coffee filter first.

Step 1: Clear cloudy water first

Dirt and debris shield germs from heat and chlorine. If the water is cloudy, let it stand until the sediment settles, then pour the clearer water off the top through a clean cloth, paper towel or coffee filter. This doesn't make the water safe, but it makes every method below work better.

Boiling: the most reliable method

  1. Bring the water to a rolling boil, the kind that keeps bubbling when you stir it.
  2. Keep it boiling for 1 minute.
  3. Let it cool naturally, then store it in clean containers with lids.

How long to boil water at high elevation

Water boils at a lower temperature higher up, so boil it for 3 minutes at elevation. The CDC sets that threshold at 6,500 feet; the EPA's page uses 5,000 feet. If you're anywhere in the mountains, 3 minutes covers both.

Boiled water tastes flat. Pouring it back and forth between two clean containers a few times puts air back into it.

Bleach: how many drops per gallon

Use regular, unscented liquid household bleach with no added cleaners, and check the label for its strength (usually 6% or 8.25% sodium hypochlorite). These are the EPA's doses:

  • Double the dose if the water is cloudy, colored or very cold.
  • Stir and wait 30 minutes before drinking.
  • The water should smell faintly of chlorine. If it doesn't, add the same dose again and wait another 15 minutes.
  • If it tastes too strongly of chlorine, pour it between clean containers and let it stand a few hours.

Purification tablets and iodine

Chlorine dioxide and other purification tablets are light and easy to carry; follow the dose and wait time on the package, which can be longer for cold or cloudy water.

With 2% tincture of iodine, the EPA's dose is 5 drops per quart or liter (10 drops if the water is cloudy or colored), then a 30-minute wait. The Minnesota Department of Health advises that iodine-treated water isn't recommended for pregnant women, people with thyroid problems or iodine sensitivity, or for continuous use beyond a few weeks.

Do water filters make water safe?

Portable filters are good at removing parasites like Giardia and Cryptosporidium and many bacteria, but most don't remove viruses; only some purifiers are rated for them, so check the label. Look for:

  • An absolute pore size of 1 micron or smaller (NSF Standard 53 or 58) for parasites
  • 0.3 microns or smaller for bacteria

Where viruses are a risk, as with water near people, livestock or sewage, filter first and then boil or disinfect. Don't drink floodwater at all (see below).

Sunlight as a last resort

With no fuel, bleach or tablets, solar disinfection (SODIS) can help with water that's already clear. Fill clean, clear plastic bottles of 2 liters or less, with the labels removed, three-quarters full, shake them, fill them to the top, and lay them in full sun for 6 hours, or 2 days if it's cloudy. It doesn't work on cloudy water, so settle and filter first.

What none of these methods fix

Boiling, bleach and filters don't remove fuel, chemicals or salt. Don't drink water that smells of fuel or chemicals, floodwater that may carry them, or pool and hot tub water. Never drink seawater or urine; they make dehydration worse. Use bottled or stored water instead.

Finding water in the wild

  • Follow the land downhill. Valleys, green vegetation and animal trails that come together often lead to water.
  • Dig in a dry streambed at its lowest point. Water may sit just below the surface.
  • Catch rain with a tarp, poncho or any clean container.
  • Collect dew before sunrise by walking through grass with cloth tied around your ankles, then wringing it out.
  • Melt snow or ice before you drink it. Eating snow chills you.

The CDC says to treat all water from outside the home, including rain. If you can't, rain caught directly in a clean container is the lowest-risk source. Clear, fast-moving water is better than stagnant water, but it still needs treating.

Hidden water at home when the taps stop

  • Your water heater: turn off its power or gas and close the cold-water valve at the top of the tank, open a hot tap somewhere in the house, and drain water from the valve at the bottom; it may be hot. Refill the tank before turning the power or gas back on. Never use water from a home-heating boiler or radiator.
  • The toilet tank, not the bowl, if you don't use tank cleaners. Treat it before drinking.
  • Ice cubes in the freezer.

Use pool or hot tub water for flushing and washing only.

How much water to store

  • At least 1 gallon per person per day for 3 daysThe CDC's minimum. A family of four needs 12 gallons.
  • A 2-week supply if you have the space
  • Extra for hot climates, pregnancy, illness and pets
  • Store-bought water: replace it by its expiration date
  • Water you bottle yourself: replace it every 6 months

Use food-grade containers with tight lids, and never ones that held chemicals like bleach or pesticides. To sanitize a container, wash and rinse it, swirl in a solution of 1 teaspoon of unscented bleach per quart of water so it touches every surface, wait at least 30 seconds, pour it out and let the container air-dry before filling it.

Quick answers

How long do you boil water to make it safe to drink?

Bring it to a rolling boil for 1 minute. At high elevation, boil it for 3 minutes: the CDC applies this above 6,500 feet and the EPA above 5,000 feet.

How much bleach do you put in a gallon of water to purify it?

The EPA's dose is 8 drops of 6% unscented household bleach, or 6 drops of 8.25% bleach, per gallon. Double it if the water is cloudy, colored or very cold, stir, and wait 30 minutes before drinking.

Do water filters remove viruses?

Most portable filters don't. They remove parasites like Giardia and Cryptosporidium and many bacteria, but only some purifiers are rated for viruses. Where viruses are a risk, filter first and then boil or disinfect.

How much water should you store for an emergency?

At least 1 gallon per person per day for 3 days, and a 2-week supply if you can, according to the CDC. Store more for hot climates, pregnancy, illness and pets, and replace water you bottle yourself every 6 months.
