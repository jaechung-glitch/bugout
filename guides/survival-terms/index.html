Survival and prepping terms, explained

Preparedness has its own vocabulary, part official and part slang. These are the terms you'll run into, each defined in a sentence or two, with a link to the guide that goes deeper.

By the BugOut team. Updated November 9, 2026. Checked against FEMA (Ready.gov), CDC, EPA and National Weather Service guidance.

The terms, A to Z

72-hour kit
Supplies to get one person through three days without outside help. The term is used for both a home emergency kit and a bug out bag packed for 72 hours.
Boil-water advisory
A notice from a water utility or health department that tap water may be contaminated. Bring water to a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 6,500 feet) before drinking it, or use bottled water. See how to make water safe to drink.
Bug in
To stay home and ride out an emergency with your own supplies instead of leaving. Also called sheltering in place.
Bug out
To leave home quickly because staying has become unsafe, such as in a wildfire, flood or hurricane evacuation.
Bug out bag (BOB)
A backpack packed in advance so one person can leave home within minutes and get by for about 72 hours. Also called a go bag or grab-and-go bag. See the bug out bag list.
Carbon monoxide (CO)
A gas with no color or smell, produced by burning fuel, that can kill. It is the main danger of running a generator, grill or camp stove indoors. Run them outdoors only, at least 20 feet from doors, windows and vents. See the power outage checklist.
Disaster supplies kit
FEMA's name for a home emergency kit: water, food and supplies for several days. See the emergency kit list.
EDC (everyday carry)
The small items a person carries every day that are useful in an emergency, such as a flashlight, a pocket knife or multi-tool, a phone charger and basic first-aid supplies.
Emergency kit
Supplies that let a household get by for several days without power, running water or stores: water, non-perishable food, light, a radio, first aid, medicines and basic tools. See the emergency kit list.
Emergency preparedness
Getting ready before a disaster so your household can stay safe and look after itself for several days: a plan, a kit and a way to receive warnings. See the emergency preparedness guide.
FIFO (first in, first out)
A way to rotate stored food: put new items at the back and use the oldest first, so nothing expires on the shelf.
Get-home bag
A small bag kept at work or in the car with what you'd need to get home on foot: water, snacks, walking shoes, a light and warm layers. It covers about 24 hours.
Go bag
Another name for a bug out bag: a packed bag you can grab on the way out the door in an evacuation.
Grid down
A long, wide-area power outage, when the electrical grid itself has failed and water, fuel, payments and phone networks can fail with it. See what to do if the power grid goes down.
Hypothermia
Dangerously low body temperature. A body temperature below 95°F is an emergency: call 911. See the signs in the winter storm guide.
INCH bag
Short for "I'm never coming home": prepper slang for a larger pack meant for leaving permanently, as opposed to a 72-hour bug out bag.
MRE (Meal, Ready-to-Eat)
A sealed, shelf-stable military field ration that can be eaten without cooking. Civilian versions are sold for camping and emergency kits.
NOAA Weather Radio
A radio service that broadcasts National Weather Service forecasts and warnings. FEMA's kit list calls for a battery-powered or hand-crank radio and a NOAA Weather Radio with tone alert.
Potable water
Water that is safe to drink. Water of unknown quality can be made potable by boiling, or by treating it with unscented bleach or purification tablets.
Prepper
Someone who prepares in advance for emergencies by storing supplies and learning skills. See prepping for beginners.
Rule of threes
A rule of thumb for survival priorities: people last about 3 minutes without air, 3 hours without shelter in harsh weather, 3 days without water and 3 weeks without food. See 12 survival skills.
Shelter in place
To stay indoors where you are, instead of evacuating, until officials say it is safe. FEMA's kit list includes plastic sheeting, scissors and duct tape for it.
SHTF
Prepper slang for the moment a serious crisis starts, as in "when things hit the fan".
STOP
What to do the moment you realize you're lost: Stop, Think, Observe, Plan. Sit down, drink, eat something and calm down before deciding anything.
Tourniquet
A band tightened around an arm or leg to stop life-threatening bleeding. Learn to use one in a Stop the Bleed® class.
Warning
A weather warning means hazardous weather is occurring, about to occur or very likely. Act now.
Watch
A weather watch means hazardous weather may occur. Get ready.
Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA)
Emergency messages sent automatically to capable mobile phones in an affected area, with no sign-up. The National Weather Service uses them for tornado, flash flood, hurricane, tsunami and other warnings.

Terms people mix up

These twoThe difference
Bug out bag and go bagNone. Same bag, two names.
Bug out bag and emergency kitThe bag is for leaving, so it is light and covers about 72 hours. The kit stays home and can hold two weeks of water and food.
Bug out bag and get-home bagA bug out bag gets you away from home. A get-home bag, kept at work or in the car, gets you back to it.
Bug in and bug outStay or go. Bug in when home is the safest place; bug out when it isn't, or when officials order an evacuation.
Watch and warningA watch means it may happen: get ready. A warning means it is happening or about to: act now.

Quick answers

What is a bug out bag?

A bug out bag is a backpack packed in advance so one person can leave home within minutes and get by for about 72 hours. It is also called a go bag or grab-and-go bag.

What is the difference between a go bag and a bug out bag?

Nothing: they are two names for the same thing, a packed bag you can grab on the way out the door in an evacuation.

What does bug in mean?

To bug in is to stay home and ride out an emergency with your own supplies, instead of leaving. Officials call it sheltering in place.

What does EDC mean?

EDC stands for everyday carry: the small, useful items a person carries every day, such as a flashlight, a multi-tool, a phone charger and basic first-aid supplies.

What does SHTF mean?

SHTF is prepper slang for the moment a serious crisis starts, as in "when things hit the fan".

What is an INCH bag?

INCH stands for "I'm never coming home". It is prepper slang for a larger pack meant for leaving permanently, as opposed to a 72-hour bug out bag.
