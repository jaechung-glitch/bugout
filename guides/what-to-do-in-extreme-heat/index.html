What to do in extreme heat

Heat waves are quiet killers: there's no wreckage, just people overheating at home. This guide covers how to stay cool, how to tell heat exhaustion from heat stroke, and what to do when someone is in danger.

By the BugOut team. Updated November 12, 2026. Checked against National Weather Service and CDC guidance.

The short answer
  1. Stay in air conditioning as much as you can. If you don't have it, spend the hottest hours at a cooling center, library or store.
  2. Drink plenty of fluids even if you aren't thirsty.
  3. Wear loose, lightweight, light-colored clothing, and plan outdoor activity for the coolest part of the day.
  4. Never leave children or pets in a parked car.
  5. Body temperature above 103°F, confusion or passing out is heat stroke: call 911 and cool the person down.

How to stay cool in a heat wave

  • Stay in an air-conditioned place as much as you can. A few hours a day helps.
  • Use a fan only if the heat index is below the high 90s. Above that, the National Weather Service says a fan can make you hotter.
  • Close blinds and curtains on sunny windows during the day.
  • Take cool showers or baths.
  • Drink fluids before you're thirsty. Limit alcohol and very sugary drinks.
  • Wear loose, lightweight, light-colored clothing and sunscreen.
  • Do outdoor work and exercise early in the morning or in the evening, and rest often in the shade.

Check on others

The CDC says older adults, young children and people with chronic medical conditions face the highest risk. Check on a friend or neighbor twice a day and have someone do the same for you, especially anyone who lives alone or has no air conditioning.

  • Never leave children or pets in a parked car, even with the windows cracked.
  • Give pets shade and plenty of water, and walk them in the cool of the day.

If the power goes out in a heat wave

Without power there's no air conditioning or fans, and your home can heat up quickly. Go to a cooling center or a home with power if you can't stay cool. Quick answers

What is the difference between heat exhaustion and heat stroke?

Heat exhaustion brings heavy sweating, weakness or tiredness, and cool, pale, clammy skin. Heat stroke is a medical emergency: body temperature above 103°F, confusion, a rapid strong pulse, or passing out. Call 911 for heat stroke.

What should you do for heat stroke?

Call 911 or get the person to a hospital right away. Move them somewhere cooler and lower their temperature with cool cloths or a cool bath. Don't give them anything to drink.

Do fans help in extreme heat?

Only up to a point. The National Weather Service says to use a fan only if the heat index is below the high 90s: above that, a fan can make you hotter. Air conditioning, even a few hours a day in a library, store or cooling center, helps far more.

Who is most at risk in a heat wave?

Older adults, young children and people with chronic medical conditions are at highest risk of heat illness and death, according to the CDC. People who work outside, live alone or have no air conditioning are also at risk.

How do you stay cool without air conditioning?

Spend the hottest hours somewhere air-conditioned, such as a cooling center or library. At home, close blinds on sunny windows, take cool showers, wear loose light-colored clothing, and drink fluids before you're thirsty.
