Emergency kit list: what to put in a home emergency kit

An emergency kit, also called a disaster supplies kit, is what your household lives on when the power, water or roads are out. This is FEMA's list in plain words, with what to add for your family and how to keep it ready.

By the BugOut team. Updated November 9, 2026. Checked against FEMA's Ready.gov Build a Kit list and CDC guidance.

The short answer

A home emergency kit needs water (1 gallon per person per day), several days of food that won't spoil, a flashlight, a battery or hand-crank radio, a first-aid kit, extra batteries, a whistle, dust masks, basic tools, a manual can opener, local maps, and a phone charger with a backup battery. Add medicines, glasses, copies of documents, cash, warm bedding, and whatever your children, pets and older relatives need. Keep it in one place and check it every six months.

What is an emergency kit?

An emergency kit is a set of supplies that lets your household get by for several days without power, running water, open stores or outside help. FEMA calls it a disaster supplies kit; you'll also see emergency preparedness kit, survival kit and 72-hour kit. They all mean the same thing: the basics, gathered in one place before you need them.

It's a home kit, meant for staying put through a storm, an outage or a boil-water notice. For leaving in a hurry, each person also needs a smaller bag: see our bug out bag list.

The basic emergency kit

This is the Basic Disaster Supplies Kit from FEMA's Ready.gov, item for item.

  • Water: 1 gallon per person per day, for several daysFor drinking and sanitation. Use our water calculator for your household's total.
  • Food: at least several days of non-perishable foodFood that needs no fridge and little or no cooking.
  • A battery-powered or hand-crank radio, and a NOAA Weather Radio with tone alertFor warnings and news when phones and power are down.
  • A flashlight
  • A first-aid kit
  • Extra batteries
  • A whistleTo signal for help.
  • Dust masksTo help filter contaminated air.
  • Plastic sheeting, scissors and duct tapeTo shelter in place.
  • Moist towelettes, garbage bags and plastic tiesFor personal sanitation.
  • A wrench or pliersTo turn off utilities.
  • A manual can opener
  • Local mapsOn paper: your phone may be dead or out of signal.
  • A cell phone with chargers and a backup battery

What to add for your household

FEMA's list of additional supplies covers the things that differ from one family to the next.

Health and hygiene

  • Prescription medicines
  • Non-prescription medicines: pain relievers, anti-diarrhea medicine, antacids or laxatives
  • Prescription glasses and contact lens solution
  • Soap, hand sanitizer and disinfecting wipes
  • Feminine supplies and personal hygiene items

Babies, children and pets

  • Infant formula, bottles, diapers, wipes and diaper rash cream
  • Books, games, puzzles or other activities for children
  • Pet food and extra water for your pet

Documents and money

  • Copies of insurance policies, identification and bank account recordsSaved electronically, or in a waterproof container you can carry.
  • Cash or traveler's checksCard readers and ATMs need power and a network.
  • Paper and pencil

Warmth, cooking and safety

  • A sleeping bag or warm blanket for each person
  • A complete change of clothing suited to your climate, and sturdy shoes
  • A fire extinguisher
  • Matches in a waterproof container
  • Mess kits, paper cups, plates, paper towels and plastic utensils

Keep it ready

  • Keep canned food in a cool, dry place
  • Store boxed food in tightly closed plastic or metal containers
  • Replace expired items as needed
  • Replace water you bottled yourself every 6 months
  • Rethink your needs every year and update the kit as your family changes

Where to keep kits

PlaceWhat FEMA suggests
HomeOne designated place, ready in case you have to leave quickly. Make sure everyone in the household knows where it is.
WorkBe ready to shelter at work for at least 24 hours: food, water, medicines and comfortable walking shoes, in a grab-and-go case.
CarA kit of emergency supplies in case you're stranded. See our car emergency kit list.

Building a kit on a budget

  • Start with what you own: flashlights, blankets, a can opener, a backpack.
  • Fill clean, food-grade containers with tap water. It's free.
  • Add one or two items to each grocery trip instead of buying everything at once.
  • Buy water, medicines and light first. Gadgets can wait.

Quick answers

What should be in an emergency kit?

FEMA's basic kit is water (1 gallon per person per day for several days), several days of non-perishable food, a battery or hand-crank radio and a NOAA Weather Radio, a flashlight, a first-aid kit, extra batteries, a whistle, dust masks, plastic sheeting with scissors and duct tape, moist towelettes with garbage bags and ties, a wrench or pliers, a manual can opener, local maps, and a cell phone with chargers and a backup battery.

How much water should be in an emergency kit?

One gallon per person per day, for drinking and sanitation. Store at least 3 days' worth and work toward 2 weeks if you have room: a family of four needs 12 gallons for 3 days and 56 gallons for 2 weeks.

How many days should an emergency kit last?

FEMA says to plan for several days, and the CDC's water guidance sets 3 days as the minimum and 2 weeks as the goal. Start with 3 days of water and food, then build up.

What is the difference between an emergency kit and a bug out bag?

An emergency kit stays at home and is for sheltering in place, so it can be large. A bug out bag, or go bag, is a backpack for each person, packed to leave home within minutes and light enough to carry, covering about 72 hours.

Where should I keep my emergency kit?

In one place at home that everyone in the household knows and can reach quickly. FEMA also suggests a kit at work, ready for at least 24 hours with food, water, medicines and comfortable walking shoes, and a kit in each car in case you're stranded.

How often should I check my emergency kit?

Look through it every six months: replace expired food and medicines, swap in fresh batteries, and replace water you bottled yourself. Rethink what your household needs once a year, since families change.
