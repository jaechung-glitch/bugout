12 survival skills everyone should know

Wilderness survival skills from the U.S. Army Survival Manual, park rangers and the CDC, in plain steps: what to do first, how to get found, and how to stay warm, hydrated and on course.

By the BugOut team. Updated November 3, 2026. Checked against U.S. Army FM 21-76, National Park Service, CDC, FCC and American College of Surgeons (Stop the Bleed) guidance.

The short answer

The most important survival skill is knowing what to do first. Get away from immediate danger, stop severe bleeding, get out of the weather, then deal with water, signaling and food, in that order. The rule of threes sets the priorities: people last about 3 minutes without air, 3 hours without shelter in harsh weather, 3 days without water and 3 weeks without food.

These 12 skills are in the order you'd usually need them. None of them replace practice: try each one once on a calm day, so it isn't the first time when it matters.

1. Carry the Ten Essentials and leave a plan

Most survival situations start with a day hike or a drive that went wrong. The National Park Service's Ten Essentials cover them:

  • Navigation: map, compass, GPS
  • Sun protection
  • Extra clothing
  • Illumination: headlamp or flashlight
  • First-aid supplies
  • Fire: matches, lighter, fire starter
  • Repair kit and tools
  • Nutrition: extra food
  • Hydration: extra water and a way to treat more
  • Emergency shelter

Before you go, tell someone where you're going and when you'll be back. Searchers start where you were last seen and along your planned route.

2. Know what to do first

  1. Get away from immediate danger: fire, floodwater, traffic, fumes, falling rock.
  2. Stop severe bleeding and make sure everyone is breathing. Call 911 if you can.
  3. Get out of the wind, wet, cold or sun. Exposure can kill in hours.
  4. Water: plan on about a gallon per person per day.
  5. Let someone know where you are, and signal for help.
  6. Food comes last. It matters least in the first 72 hours.

Conditions shift the order: in the cold, shelter and fire come before water; in the desert, water and shade come first.

3. Stop when you're lost

Our full guide: what to do if you get lost in the woods.

The moment you realize you're lost, stop walking. Use STOP: Stop, Think, Observe, Plan. Sit down, drink, eat something and calm down before deciding anything.

  • Call 911 if you have any signal. If a call won't connect, try texting 911; text-to-911 isn't available everywhere, and you'll get a bounce-back message if it isn't. Turn off what you don't need to save battery.
  • Once someone knows you need help, stay put. Move only if staying is dangerous.
  • Teach children to "hug a tree": stay with one tree, blow a whistle and answer anyone calling their name.

4. Signal for help

Three of anything means distress: three whistle blasts, three fires, three shouts, three flashes.

  • Whistle: three blasts, pause, repeat. It carries much farther than your voice.
  • Light at night: SOS, three short, three long, three short flashes.
  • Mirror or anything shiny: aim the flash at aircraft or distant searchers.
  • Ground signals: in an open area, lay out a large X (need medical help) or V (need help) with rocks, logs or bright fabric.
  • Be visible: spread bright clothing or a tarp in a clearing. Aircraft can't see you under trees.

5. Build an emergency shelter

Cold, wind and wet can kill in hours, so shelter usually comes before water or food.

  • Stay dry. Wet clothes, from rain or sweat, pull heat out fast. Take off layers before you start sweating.
  • Get out of the wind behind rocks, a fallen tree or thick brush, but not at the bottom of a valley, where cold air settles at night, and not under dead branches.
  • Insulate yourself from the ground with a pad, pack, branches or a thick layer of dry leaves. The ground steals more heat than the air.
  • Build small: tie a tarp or poncho into a lean-to, or build a debris hut just big enough for your body and pile leaves on it at least 3 feet thick.
  • If you're stranded in a car, stay with it. It's shelter, and it's easier to spot than a person.

Start building well before dark.

6. Start a fire

Most fires fail at the tinder. Gather all three sizes before you strike anything:

  • Tinder: dry, fine and fluffy material: dry grass, birch bark, pine needles, cotton balls, dryer lint.
  • Kindling: dry twigs from pencil-lead to pencil thickness. Dead branches still on a tree are the driest.
  • Fuel: wrist-thick wood and larger.

Clear a spot down to bare soil, light the tinder, lay kindling over it in a small teepee with gaps for air, and blow gently at the base. Add bigger wood only as each size catches. With no matches or lighter, a ferro rod, a 9-volt battery touched to fine steel wool, or a magnifying lens in bright sun can light tinder. Never leave a fire unattended, and drown it until the ashes are cold.

7. Find and purify water

Follow valleys downhill, dig at the lowest point of a dry streambed, catch rain, and melt snow rather than eating it. Then treat it: a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes at high elevation), or unscented bleach at 8 drops per gallon of 6% bleach, doubled for cloudy water, with a 30-minute wait. Our water guide has the full doses, filters and what never works.

8. Find north without a compass

  • Shadow-stick method: push a straight stick about 3 feet long into level ground and mark the tip of its shadow. Wait 10 to 15 minutes and mark the new tip. The first mark is west and the second is east. Stand with the first mark on your left and the second on your right: you're facing north.
  • The North Star: find the Big Dipper. The two stars at the end of its cup point to the North Star, about five times the distance between them. It sits almost exactly over north.
  • The sun: it rises in the east and sets in the west. In the continental U.S., the midday sun is due south.

9. Hold a direction and use your phone's GPS

Your phone's GPS works without cell service; only the map tiles need data, so download offline maps before you go. To walk a straight line, pick a landmark ahead, walk to it, then pick the next one. If you're unsure, go back to the last place you knew where you were rather than pushing on.

10. Stop severe bleeding

Severe bleeding can kill in minutes, faster than help can arrive. The American College of Surgeons' Stop the Bleed steps:

  1. Call 911, or have someone else call.
  2. Arm or leg, and you have a tourniquet: put it on right away, 2 to 3 inches above the wound and never on a joint. Tighten it until the bleeding stops and note the time. Leave removing it to medical staff.
  3. No tourniquet, or the wound is on the neck, shoulder or groin: pack a large, deep wound with gauze or clean cloth until it's filled, then press hard with both hands.
  4. Otherwise, press: cover the wound with gauze or a clean cloth and push down as hard as you can with both hands until help takes over.

A commercial tourniquet works far better than a belt or cord. Most Stop the Bleed® classes take 90 minutes or less and teach this hands-on.

11. Recognize hypothermia and heat illness

Hypothermia

The CDC's warning signs: shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech and drowsiness. In babies: bright red, cold skin and very low energy. A body temperature below 95°F needs medical attention right away.

  • Get the person somewhere warm and out of the wind, and take off any wet clothing.
  • Warm the center of the body first, the chest, neck, head and groin, under dry blankets and layers.
  • Handle them gently. Give warm drinks only if they're fully awake, and no alcohol.
  • Get medical care as soon as possible. If they aren't breathing, start CPR and keep warming them.

Heat stroke and heat exhaustion

Heat stroke is an emergency: a body temperature of 103°F or higher, hot red skin, a fast pulse, confusion or passing out. Call 911, move the person somewhere cooler and cool them with cool water, wet cloths or a cool bath. Don't give them anything to drink.

Heat exhaustion brings heavy sweating, cold clammy skin, nausea, cramps and dizziness. Move to a cool place, loosen clothing, use cool wet cloths and sip water. Get medical help if the person is throwing up, getting worse, or not better within an hour.

12. Find food safely

You can last weeks without food, so it comes after water and shelter, and you should eat little if you have little water.

  • Fish and small game are the safest wild foods. Cook all meat and fish thoroughly.
  • Insects like grasshoppers, crickets and grubs are plentiful protein. Cook them, and avoid any that sting or bite, are hairy or brightly colored, or smell strongly.
  • Never eat wild mushrooms. Deadly ones look like safe ones.
  • Eat only plants you can identify with certainty. Avoid any with milky sap, an almond smell, seeds in pods, or leaves like carrot or parsley; several deadly plants look like that.

Quick answers

What is the most important survival skill?

Knowing what to do first. Get away from immediate danger, stop severe bleeding, get out of the weather, then deal with water, signaling for help and food, in that order.

What is the rule of threes in survival?

A way to set priorities: people last about 3 minutes without air, 3 hours without shelter in harsh weather, 3 days without water and 3 weeks without food.

What should you do if you get lost in the woods?

Stop walking and use STOP: Stop, Think, Observe, Plan. Call 911 if you have any signal, or try texting 911 where it's available. Then stay where you are once someone knows you need help, make yourself easy to see, and signal with three whistle blasts.

What are the Ten Essentials?

The National Park Service's list for any outing: navigation, sun protection, extra clothing, illumination, first-aid supplies, fire, a repair kit and tools, extra food, extra water and emergency shelter.
