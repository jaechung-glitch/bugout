The short answer

Pack enough to get each person through 72 hours on their own: drinking water and a way to purify more, three days of food that needs no cooking, a flashlight, a radio, a first-aid kit and your medicines, a whistle, masks, warm layers and rain gear, a phone charger and power bank, copies of your IDs and some cash, plus anything your kids, pets or older relatives need. Keep one bag per person by the door and check it every six months.

What is a bug out bag?

A bug out bag, also called a go bag, grab-and-go bag or 72-hour kit, is a backpack packed in advance so you can leave home within minutes during a wildfire, flood, hurricane evacuation, gas leak or house fire. The idea comes from the assumption FEMA builds its own kit list on: in a disaster, help may not reach you for several days, so you should be able to take care of yourself until it does.

It is different from the supplies you keep at home. A home kit is for sheltering in place and can be large: two weeks of water and food if you have the space. A bug out bag has to be light enough to carry, so it covers the essentials for about three days and relies on a way to purify water rather than carrying all of it.

The bug out bag checklist

Work through it category by category. Print the page to tick items off as you pack.

Water

  • Water bottles or a hydration bladder, 2 to 3 liters per personA gallon of water weighs about 8.3 pounds, so carry some and plan to purify the rest.
  • A way to make water safe: a portable filter plus purification tablets, or plain unscented bleach and a dropperMost portable filters don't remove viruses, so pair a filter with tablets or bleach.
  • A collapsible water container for collecting and treating water

Food

  • Three days of food that needs no cooking: energy or protein bars, nut butter packets, jerky, dried fruit, nuts, ready-to-eat meals
  • A manual can opener if you pack any cans, and a spoon
  • Food for special diets, babies and pets

Shelter and warmth

  • An emergency blanket or bivy sack
  • A rain poncho or rain jacket
  • A warm layer and a hat, even in summer
  • A sleeping bag or warm blanket if you can carry it
  • Work gloves

Light, power and communication

  • A flashlight or headlamp, plus extra batteries
  • A battery-powered or hand-crank radio, ideally a NOAA Weather Radio
  • A whistle to signal for helpThree blasts is the distress signal, and a whistle carries much farther than your voice.
  • A phone charging cable and a charged power bank
  • A written list of phone numbers and a paper map of your areaYour phone may be dead or out of signal when you need them.

First aid and health

  • A first-aid kit: bandages, gauze, tape, antiseptic wipes, gloves, and a tourniquet if you know how to use one
  • Prescription medicines, with a written list of names and doses
  • Over-the-counter basics: pain reliever, antihistamine, anti-diarrhea medicine
  • Glasses or contact lenses and solution
  • N95 or dust masksFor smoke, ash and dust after fires, storms and earthquakes.
  • Hand sanitizer and moist towelettes

Tools

  • A multi-tool or knife
  • Matches in a waterproof container and a lighter
  • Duct tape and a length of cord
  • A wrench or pliers to turn off utilities

Documents and money

  • Copies of IDs, insurance policies and prescriptions in a waterproof bag
  • Cash in small billsCard readers and ATMs need power and a network.
  • Emergency contacts, including someone out of town

Clothing and hygiene

  • A complete change of clothes and sturdy shoes
  • Extra socks
  • Toilet paper, garbage bags and plastic ties for sanitation
  • Toothbrush, soap and feminine supplies

For the people and pets who depend on you

  • Babies: formula, bottles, diapers, wipes
  • Older adults: hearing aid batteries, mobility aids, extra glasses
  • Pets: food, water, leash or carrier, and vaccination records

How much water and food to pack

FEMA and the CDC both plan around one gallon of water per person per day, for drinking and basic hygiene. For 72 hours that's three gallons each, about 25 pounds, which is too heavy for most people to carry far. That's why a bug out bag carries a few liters and the means to treat more.

SupplyPer personHow to carry it
Water1 gallon a day (3 gallons for 72 hours)2 to 3 liters in the bag, the rest treated on the way or kept in the car
Food3 daysCalorie-dense, no-cook food. Food labels use 2,000 calories a day as a reference.
MedicinesEnough to last until you can reach a pharmacyIn original bottles, with a written list of doses

To treat water on the move, a rolling boil for one minute is the most reliable method. Without a stove, use bleach: the EPA's dose is 8 drops of 6% unscented bleach per gallon (6 drops of 8.25% bleach), doubled for cloudy water, then a 30-minute wait. Choosing the bag

  • A backpack, not a duffel. You may have to walk. Padded shoulder straps and a hip belt make a loaded pack bearable.
  • Light enough to walk a few miles with. Pack it, put it on, and walk around the block. If that's hard, take things out.
  • Waterproof inside. Line the bag with a heavy garbage bag and keep documents, medicines and electronics in zip-top bags.
  • Plain-looking. A bag that looks ordinary is less likely to attract attention in a crowded shelter.

Where to keep it and when to check it

  • At home: by the door you'd leave through, or in a hall closet, where everyone in the house knows to find it.
  • In the car: a smaller kit with water, snacks, a blanket, a flashlight and a first-aid kit, in case you're stranded.
  • At work: FEMA suggests being ready to shelter there for at least 24 hours, with food, water, medicines and comfortable walking shoes.

Check the bag every six months. Replace water you bottled yourself (the CDC says every six months), rotate food before it expires, swap in fresh batteries, update medicines and clothes for the season, and reassess what your family needs once a year.

Common bug out bag mistakes

  • Packing too heavy. A 50-pound bag you can't carry is worse than a 20-pound bag you can.
  • One bag for the whole family. Each person who can carry a pack should have one, so splitting up doesn't leave anyone without water or medicine.
  • Forgetting medicines and glasses. They're the hardest things to replace in a disaster.
  • No cash and no paper. Plan for a dead phone: written phone numbers, a paper map and small bills.
  • Packing and forgetting. Dead batteries and expired food are common. Put the six-month check on your calendar.
  • Never practicing. Walk your evacuation route once with the bag on, and make sure everyone knows where the bags are.
