The short answer
  1. Find out how big the outage is from a battery or hand-crank radio, and expect it to last longer than a normal one.
  2. Fill bathtubs, pots and jugs with water while the taps still run. Water pressure can fail once the utility's backup power runs out.
  3. Keep the fridge and freezer shut, eat the fridge food first, then the freezer, then the pantry.
  4. Save your phone battery, text instead of calling, and keep cash on hand: card readers, ATMs and gas pumps need power.
  5. Never run a generator, grill or camp stove indoors. If your home gets dangerously hot or cold, go to a warming or cooling center.

What does "grid down" mean?

"Grid down" is the prepper term for a power outage that hits a whole region rather than one street, and lasts days or weeks instead of hours. Causes include hurricanes, ice storms and extreme cold or heat that overload or damage power plants and lines.

The difference matters because almost everything else depends on electricity. In a short outage you can wait it out. When the grid is down, water, sewer service, fuel, payments and phone networks can fail one after another, so you need a plan for each.

Most outages end within a day, but a regional one can run much longer. The Red Cross suggests storing food and water for about two weeks. If you can get through two weeks at home, you can ride out nearly any outage.

What to do in the first 24 hours

  1. Find out how big it is. Turn on a battery or hand-crank radio, or check your utility's outage map while your phone has signal. A whole region dark means plan for days.
  2. Fill water containers now. Fill bathtubs (for flushing and washing), pots and clean jugs (for drinking) while there's still pressure.
  3. Keep the fridge and freezer closed. Food stays safe about 4 hours in a closed fridge, 48 hours in a full freezer and 24 hours in a half-full one, according to the USDA.
  4. Switch to flashlights and headlamps. Candles are a fire risk, and the fire department may be stretched thin.
  5. Unplug electronics. Power can return in surges. Leave one lamp on so you know when it's back.
  6. Save phone battery. Low Power Mode, screen dimmed, text instead of calling. Charge from a power bank or a running car outdoors.
  7. Start the medical backup plan. Anyone who relies on oxygen, a CPAP, dialysis or refrigerated medicine should act now, not when batteries run low. The Red Cross says to evacuate if you depend on powered medical devices and can't run them.
  8. Check on neighbors, especially older people living alone, and agree how you'll keep in touch.

How to get water when the grid is down

  • Stored water first. The CDC says to store 1 gallon per person per day, at least 3 days' worth and 2 weeks if you can. Our water calculator works out your household's total.
  • Water already in your home. The CDC lists your water heater tank, melted ice cubes, and the toilet tank (not the bowl) if the water is clear and has no cleaning chemicals in it.
  • Treat anything else. Boil it or disinfect it with unscented bleach; our water guide has the doses. Never drink water that may have fuel or chemicals in it: boiling won't fix that.
  • Follow local notices. When pressure returns, your utility may issue a boil-water notice until it has tested the water.

How to eat when the power is out for days

  • Eat in this order: the fridge first (safe about 4 hours with the door closed), then the freezer, then the pantry.
  • Throw out perishable food that has been at 40°F or warmer for two hours or more. Never taste food to test it.
  • Keep at least two weeks of food that needs no cooking: canned meat, fish and beans, peanut butter, crackers, cereal, dried fruit and nuts. Don't forget a manual can opener.
  • Cook only outdoors on a grill or camp stove, never indoors or in a garage.

How to stay warm or cool without power

  • In the cold: keep everyone in one small room with the doors shut, block drafts, hang blankets over windows at night, and wear layers, hats and dry socks. Let faucets drip if pipes could freeze.
  • In the heat: spend the hottest hours somewhere with air conditioning, drink water before you're thirsty, and close blinds on sunny windows.
  • Never heat your home with a gas oven or stove, or run a generator, grill or camp stove inside, even with windows open. Carbon monoxide has no smell; put battery-powered alarms near bedrooms.
  • If you can't keep your home safe, go to a warming or cooling center, or stay with family who have power. Babies and older adults are most at risk.

How to stay in touch and get news

  • A battery-powered or hand-crank radio, ideally a NOAA Weather Radio, is the most reliable way to hear warnings and updates.
  • Keep a paper list of phone numbers: your phone may die, and you won't remember them.
  • Agree on a meeting place and an out-of-area contact everyone checks in with. Long-distance calls and texts sometimes get through when local ones don't.
  • Look for community charging stations; officials often announce them on the radio.

Medicines and medical devices

  • Keep a supply of prescription medicines and ask your doctor or pharmacist how each one should be stored without power.
  • The FDA says insulin in its original vial or pen can usually be kept unrefrigerated between 59°F and 86°F for up to 28 days. Never use insulin that has frozen.
  • Keep charged backup batteries for oxygen concentrators, CPAPs and power wheelchairs, and ask your utility about its medical priority program.

When to leave home

Staying home, or bugging in, is usually safest. Leave if officials tell you to, if your home becomes too hot or too cold to be safe, if someone's medical equipment can't be powered, or if you run out of water. Go before roads and fuel get scarce, and take a go bag for each person.
