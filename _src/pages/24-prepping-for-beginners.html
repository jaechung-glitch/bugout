The short answer

Start with the emergencies most likely where you live, then build in this order: a household plan, water (1 gallon per person per day, 3 days at minimum and 2 weeks if you can), food that needs no cooking, a go bag for each person, first aid and medicines, and power outage basics. Add skills and longer-term supplies once those are in place.

What prepping actually means

For most households, prepping means being able to look after yourselves for a few days to two weeks after something goes wrong: a power outage, a winter storm, a hurricane, a flood, a wildfire evacuation or an earthquake. FEMA's own planning assumes help may not reach everyone right away, so the goal is to cover your basic needs until it does.

It doesn't take a bunker or a big budget. A plan, some water and food, a bag by the door and a few skills cover most of what you're likely to face.

Before you start: know your risks

  • Look up your area. FEMA's National Risk Index rates every U.S. county and census tract for 18 natural hazards, from hurricanes and tornadoes to wildfire, earthquakes and winter weather.
  • Sign up for alerts. Make sure Emergency Alerts are on in your phone's settings, and sign up for your county's local alert system.
  • Get a NOAA Weather Radio or a battery or hand-crank radio, for when phones and power are down.

Your list of likely events shapes everything else: hurricane country needs an evacuation plan, cold climates need a way to stay warm without power, earthquake zones need sturdy shoes by the bed.

Week 1: Make a plan

Ready.gov's planning steps start with five questions to talk through with your household:

  1. How will we get emergency alerts and warnings?
  2. Where will we shelter at home, for a tornado, a storm or a chemical spill?
  3. What's our evacuation route, and where would we go?
  4. How will we reach each other if we're apart and phones are down?
  5. Do we need to update our emergency supplies?
  • Pick an out-of-town contact everyone can textTexts often get through when calls don't, and a long-distance contact may be easier to reach than someone local.
  • Choose two meeting places: one near home, one outside your neighborhood
  • Write down phone numbers on paper for everyone's wallet or bag
  • Plan for anyone with medical needs, young children, older relatives and pets
  • Learn how to shut off your water, gas and electricity
  • Practice the plan once, including walking or driving your evacuation route

Week 2: Water and food

Water

  • 1 gallon per person per day for at least 3 daysThe CDC's minimum, for drinking, cooking and brushing teeth. A family of four needs 12 gallons.
  • Work toward a 2-week supply
  • Extra for pets, hot weather, pregnancy and illness
  • A way to treat more: unscented bleach, purification tablets or a filter

Food

  • At least 3 days of food that needs no refrigeration and little or no cooking
  • Canned meat, fish, beans, soup, fruit and vegetables
  • Peanut butter, crackers, cereal, nuts, dried fruit, granola bars, shelf-stable milk
  • A manual can opener
  • Food for babies, special diets and pets

Store what you already eat and eat what you store: put new cans at the back, use the oldest first, and you'll never be left with a closet of expired food. Rice, dried beans and pasta keep for years but need water and fuel to cook, so add them once the no-cook basics are covered.

Week 3: Go bags and first aid

  • A go bag for each person, packed for 72 hoursOur bug out bag list has the full checklist.
  • A first-aid kit at home and one in each car
  • A supply of prescription medicines and a written list of doses and allergies
  • Copies of IDs, insurance and medical information in a waterproof bag
  • Cash in small bills
  • Sign up for a first-aid and CPR class

Week 4: Power, light and heat

  • A flashlight or headlamp for each person, plus batteries
  • Charged power banks and a car phone charger
  • Battery-powered carbon monoxide alarms on every level, and tested smoke alarms
  • Warm blankets or sleeping bags for a winter outage
  • Appliance thermometers and a cooler for food safety
  • A plan for medical devices that need electricity

Read our power outage checklist for what to do once the lights go out, including the generator rules that prevent carbon monoxide poisoning.

After the basics: skills and practice

Supplies run out; skills don't. Once the four weeks are done, practice a few things on a calm day:

  • Cook a meal without power, on a camp stove outdoors
  • Treat a gallon of water with bleach, using the right dose for your bottle
  • Find north with the shadow-stick method
  • Live for one evening on your supplies alone, and note what you missed

Our guide to 12 survival skills covers the rest, from signaling for help to stopping severe bleeding.

Prepping on a budget

  • Add one or two items to each grocery trip instead of buying everything at once.
  • Fill clean, food-grade containers with tap water for free, and replace it every 6 months.
  • Use what you already own: camping gear, old backpacks, spare blankets.
  • Spend first on water, medicines and light. Skip gadgets until the basics are done.
  • The plan, the skills and the paperwork cost nothing.

Prepper apps worth having

A phone is one of the most useful tools in an emergency, as long as what's on it works without a network:

  • BugOut keeps survival guides, disaster checklists and the U.S. Army Survival Manual offline, with an AI assistant that answers questions on the phone itself.
  • The FEMA app sends weather alerts and shows open shelters and disaster resources, when you have a connection.
  • Your county's alert system and your utility's app give local warnings and outage updates.
  • Offline maps in your maps app, downloaded for your area and evacuation route before you need them.
