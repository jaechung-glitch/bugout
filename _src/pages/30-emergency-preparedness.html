The short answer

Do three things, in this order. Make a plan: agree how you'll get warnings, where you'll shelter, where you'll go if you have to leave, and how you'll reach each other. Build a kit: at least 3 days of water (1 gallon per person per day), food that won't spoil, light, a radio, first aid and medicines. Stay informed: keep emergency alerts on and own a battery or hand-crank radio. The same three steps cover almost any disaster.

What emergency preparedness means

Emergency preparedness, or disaster preparedness, is what you do before something goes wrong so that your household can stay safe and look after itself until help arrives or services come back. FEMA's planning assumes help may not reach everyone right away, so the aim is to cover your own basic needs for several days.

You don't need a different plan for every disaster. A power outage, a winter storm, a hurricane, a flood and an earthquake all come down to the same questions: do we stay or go, do we have water, food, light and medicine, and how do we find out what's happening?

Step 0: Know what's likely where you live

  • Look up your county. FEMA's National Risk Index rates every U.S. county and census tract for 18 natural hazards, from hurricanes and tornadoes to wildfire, earthquakes and winter weather.
  • Ask your local emergency management office which events it plans for and where the evacuation routes and shelters are.

Your list shapes the details: hurricane country needs an evacuation plan, cold climates need a way to stay warm without power.

Step 1: Make a family emergency plan

Ready.gov's planning steps start with questions to talk through with your household:

  1. How will we get emergency alerts and warnings?
  2. Where will we shelter at home, for a tornado, a storm or a chemical spill?
  3. What's our evacuation route, and where would we go?
  4. How will we reach each other if we're apart and phones are down?
  5. Do we need to update our emergency supplies?
  • Pick an out-of-town contact everyone can textTexts often get through when calls don't.
  • Choose two meeting places: one near home, one outside your neighborhood
  • Write phone numbers on paper for every wallet and bag
  • Plan for young children, older relatives, pets and anyone with medical needs
  • Plan for medical devices that need electricity
  • Learn how to shut off your water, gas and electricity
  • Practice once, including walking or driving your evacuation route

Step 2: Build an emergency kit

A home kit covers several days without power, water or stores. The core of FEMA's list:

  • Water: 1 gallon per person per day. Store at least 3 days and work toward 2 weeks.
  • Food: several days of food that needs no fridge and little or no cooking, plus a manual can opener.
  • Light and news: a flashlight, extra batteries, and a battery or hand-crank radio.
  • Health: a first-aid kit, prescription medicines and glasses.
  • Everything else: a whistle, dust masks, basic tools, local maps, a phone charger with a backup battery, copies of documents and cash.

Our emergency kit list has the full checklist to print, and the water calculator works out the amounts for your household. If you may have to leave home fast, pack a bug out bag for each person, and keep a kit in the car.

Step 3: Stay informed

  • Keep emergency alerts turned on in your phone's settingsWireless Emergency Alerts reach capable phones automatically, with no sign-up. The National Weather Service uses them for tornado, flash flood, hurricane, tsunami and other warnings.
  • Sign up for your county's local alert system
  • Own a battery or hand-crank radio, ideally a NOAA Weather RadioIt works when the power and cell networks don't.

Keep it up

  • Every six months: replace expired food, medicines and batteries, and water you bottled yourself.
  • Every year: rethink what your household needs and update the plan and the kit.
  • Take a first-aid and CPR class. Supplies run out; skills don't.
